How I work

Curious first. Clear close behind.

Three principles guide how I run research and how I show up in the room — whether or not I hold the title that's supposed to make people listen.

I listen before I frame.

Every study starts with the people closest to the problem — users and stakeholders alike — long enough to understand what they're actually weighing.

I make sense of it together.

I design synthesis sessions and workshops so the team reaches the insight together, in the room, rather than reading it off a slide.

I keep the work moving.

A simple framework the team actually uses beats a comprehensive one that sits in a deck. I build for the decision in front of us.

Why I start with listening

Most research goes sideways before a single participant is recruited, because the team never agreed on what decision the work is actually for. I start every project by getting stakeholders to say, out loud, what they're trying to decide and why it matters. That single conversation does more to keep a study useful than any method I could choose afterward.

This is also where mentoring shapes how I work. Through my mentoring at the University of Michigan, I've watched new researchers learn the same lesson I had to learn myself: a perfectly executed study that answers the wrong question helps no one. Curiosity about the actual problem, not the assumed one, is the place to begin.

Why shared understanding matters more than a polished deck

A finding I deliver alone tends to get nodded at in a meeting and forgotten by the following week. A finding a cross-functional room arrives at together gets built into the roadmap, because the team now owns it. That's why I spend as much energy designing the synthesis session as I do designing the study itself — playtest viewings, working sessions, and informal show-and-tells where people can sit with the data themselves.

Why I value decisiveness over exhaustiveness

I'd rather hand a team a usable framework in week one than a comprehensive one in month three. Most of the impact comes from giving people a shared, simple way to talk about a problem — not from covering every edge case. I look for the smallest tool that will actually get used, and I'd rather refine it later than wait for it to be perfect now.
