How I lead

Warm enough to nurture. Clear enough to move things.

I've led research across gaming studios, academic publishing, and enterprise technology. These are the principles that show up regardless of the context.

Nurturing

I want the people around me to feel supported, seen, and capable of more than they thought. That shows up in how I give feedback, how I hold space for uncertainty, and how I celebrate progress as much as outcomes. A team that feels cared for does better work — and I've seen that play out enough times to build my whole approach around it.

Leaning in with curiosity

My instinct when something's unclear isn't to fill the gap with assumptions — it's to ask more questions. I find ambiguity genuinely interesting. It usually means there's something worth understanding underneath. I've found that curiosity is contagious in a room: when a researcher approaches a problem with open, genuine interest, the people around them tend to start doing the same.

Building alignment and shared understanding

The most important thing I can do on any project isn't produce a finding — it's make sure the people who need to act on it are already moving in the same direction before the readout happens. I design synthesis sessions and working workshops so teams reach insight together, not just receive it. I'm often described as the person in the room who gets everyone speaking the same language.

Transparency

I'd rather name what I don't know than project confidence I haven't earned. Teams work better when everyone can see the reasoning, not just the output — when uncertainty is acknowledged openly instead of papered over. I share my thinking early and often, including the parts that are still rough. That kind of openness tends to make everyone around me more willing to do the same.

Mentoring and coaching

I've mentored graduate students at the University of Michigan and served as a formal mentor at JSTOR, where my mentee was promoted from Associate User Researcher to User Researcher. Helping someone grow into their own voice as a researcher — watching them trust their instincts and advocate for their work — is some of the most satisfying work I do. I take it seriously because someone did that for me.

Authoritative, but never authoritarian

I know this work deeply and I'll advocate for a direction when I believe in it. But the goal is never compliance — it's understanding. I'd rather someone push back and tell me why than agree without knowing why. Authority in research comes from demonstrated judgment, not from holding the title. I try to earn that trust every time, rather than assuming it.

The thread that connects all of it

Most of what I care about as a leader comes back to one idea: people do their best work when they feel genuinely understood. That's true for the users I research, the researchers I mentor, and the cross-functional teams I work alongside. My job — whether I'm running a study, facilitating a synthesis session, or coaching a junior researcher through their first stakeholder presentation — is to help people feel clear, capable, and pointed in the same direction.

Why I invest so heavily in mentoring

I'm a first-generation college graduate, and I carry a deep sense of gratitude toward the people who made room for me at tables I didn't expect to sit at. That shapes how I think about mentoring — not as an add-on to the job, but as one of the reasons I'm here. When I worked with my mentee at JSTOR, what I was really trying to do was give her the confidence to trust her own expertise sooner than I trusted mine. Watching her get promoted felt like the research landed.

What "building shared understanding" actually looks like

It means I spend as much time designing the conversation around research as I spend designing the research itself. It means I run workshops where the team reaches the finding together rather than hears it from me in a slide deck. It means I ask stakeholders to say, out loud, what decision the work is for — before a single participant is recruited. It's the part of the process most people skip, and it's the part that determines whether the work gets used.
