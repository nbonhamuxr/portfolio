ELECTRONIC ARTS / MAXIS

How I got a studio known only for single-player experiences to build for a mobile-first, multiplayer audience

An eight-month initiative combining original research, competitive analysis, and a four-part studio-wide series that reframed who Maxis was building for — and what the game needed to become.

My Role
Sr. Researcher + Co-Author
Duration
8 months
Reach
Studio-wide: product, design, and leadership
The Studio

A studio built on single-player, asked to build for a multiplayer world

Maxis had spent decades perfecting one thing: the single-player life simulation. The Sims franchise was built on private creative worlds — your Sims, your story, your pace. When the studio began work on a new multiplayer-first title targeting a younger audience, that instinct didn't disappear. Neither did the skepticism about what they were being asked to learn from.

Roblox was everywhere, undeniably enormous, and easy to write off. The art looked cheap. The mechanics felt basic. The studio's existing approach to competitive research — feature audits, catalogues of what competitor titles had — wasn't built to answer the questions that actually mattered. It answered "what does the competition have," not "why do players choose to spend their time there."

The Problem

Everyone knew Roblox mattered. No one knew why.

There was no shared picture of the audience Maxis was building for. Leadership knew Roblox was culturally significant to younger players — but that knowledge lived at the level of awareness, not design conviction. Strategic conversations stayed at features and aesthetics rather than player motivation.

The more pressing issue: the game had no defined core loop. The direction was "life sim, but multiplayer" — a starting point, not a design foundation. Without a grounded understanding of what multiplayer actually meant to the players they were trying to reach, the studio was designing from assumption. The business question wasn't "how do we beat Roblox" — it was "what does this audience tell us about who we're building for and what they need?"

Building For Our Players

Setting the stage before the research ran

Before any Roblox-specific research existed, I co-authored a four-part studio-wide series called "Building For Our Players" alongside the Sr. Creative Director, Experience Design Director, and Lead Game PM. Presented to the full studio over several months, the series was designed to build shared understanding of who we were building for — and what it would take to reach them.

My segment, "What We've Learned," synthesized early research findings: storyboard testing, art style evaluations, motivational profiles, and an emerging picture of what this new audience actually expected. The series established shared vocabulary, defined methodological approaches, and gave cross-functional teams an early read on where the data was pointing.

The series moved the needle. But even four installments in, the studio's understanding of Roblox players specifically stayed surface-level. Knowing who to build for is different from knowing what they actually do, and why they keep coming back.

The Roblox Study

I ran this research on my own initiative

The Roblox research wasn't commissioned. I saw a gap in the studio's understanding and decided to fill it. I designed ride-along interviews: nine 60-minute sessions with teenagers and young adults who already played tycoon-style games on Roblox. Participants shared their screens while playing games they knew well, narrating their experience in their own words. Each session closed with a co-design exercise asking players to imagine what a tycoon experience built specifically for them would feel like.

I combined these sessions with secondary research, consumer insights reports, motivational profiles, and telemetry — building a layered picture of why these players were on Roblox, not just what they were doing there.

Getting the research in front of the team required more than a readout. When I moderated a live session with developers during an onsite — playing Roblox together as a group — the reaction was almost uniformly dismissive. The art looked amateurish. The mechanics felt basic. The team was stuck on first impressions and couldn't get past the aesthetics to find what was actually working. Getting them there — to see why this "cheap-looking" platform had an enormous, loyal, deeply engaged player base — was as much the work as the research itself.

Why players keep coming back to Roblox

Varied

  • There's always something new — games, updates, events
  • Something for whatever mood I'm in
  • I can play for a round or for hours
  • Cooperative, spectating, parallel play — multiple ways to be in the same space

The findings reframed the competitive question entirely. This wasn't about feature parity. Players didn't want a higher-fidelity Roblox. What they wanted was a game that understood what Roblox had taught them to expect from a shared experience.

"It isn't about making a higher fidelity version of Roblox — in fact their players don't really care about that much. Instead, it's about understanding the motivations, mental models, and behaviors of an audience that grew up playing Roblox so that we can build an exemplary experience that delights and retains that audience."

What Shifted

From life-sim instinct to a multiplayer-first core loop

Before this work, the game had no defined core loop. "Life sim, but multiplayer" was a starting point — not a design foundation. The research gave the team something concrete to build from: a player-grounded framework for what a multiplayer experience actually needed to do for this audience.

What emerged was a three-part structure that replaced the old solo life-sim model entirely:

Pillar 01

Play Together

Minigames, exploration, and shared activities — things to do with friends right now, without setup or waiting.

Pillar 02

Build Together

Co-building and decorating shared spaces — creative collaboration with something to show for it.

Pillar 03

Live Together

A communal life-sim experience — shared narrative, shared space, shared identity over time.

This reframe — from private creative world to shared living experience — became the organizing principle for the game's design direction. It gave the studio a framework for evaluating feature decisions against player behavior rather than competitor feature lists.

The Enduring Impact

The framing outlasted the study

Eight months after the first Building For Our Players installment, the studio had a shared picture of its audience, a defined core loop built around multiplayer and mobile, and a methodology for competitive research that answered design questions instead of cataloguing features.

Framing

The Roblox and tycoon frameworks are still actively referenced by the team's principal designer — the framing outlasted the study by years.

Studio

The studio's relationship with Roblox shifted from dismissal to genuine curiosity — developers who once asked "why would anyone play this?" began using it as a reference point for player expectations.

Methodology

EA leadership adopted this player-centered, decision-driving approach company-wide, replacing the feature audit methodology that had been the standard.
