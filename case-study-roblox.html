Background

At Maxis, competitive research typically meant broad feature audits â€” comprehensive catalogues of what competitor titles had. Teams produced thorough reports, but they rarely changed what got built, because they answered "what does the competition have" rather than "why do players choose to spend their time there." Roblox was on everyone's radar. Tycoon-style games on the platform had a massive, dedicated player base â€” many of them in the same demographic Maxis was trying to attract to The Sims 4. The studio needed to understand what was working, and why, in terms players would actually recognize.

The Problem

Leadership knew Roblox was culturally significant to younger players, but the team's understanding of it was surface-level. Strategic conversations stayed at the level of features and aesthetics rather than player motivation. Without a grounded picture of why players kept coming back, the studio risked designing for assumptions instead of behavior. The business question wasn't "how do we beat Roblox?" â€” it was "what can Roblox teach us about the players we're trying to reach?"

My Approach

I started not with a study, but with the team. I organized a studio-wide "let's play together" session â€” cross-functional groups playing Roblox together, using affinity mapping to surface assumptions, identify confusion, and name motivation gaps. This built genuine shared curiosity before a single participant was recruited, and it created trust for the research that followed. Then, rather than using EA's standard playtest methodology â€” questionnaires, structured evaluations â€” I designed ride-along interviews: nine 60-minute sessions with teens and young adults who already played tycoon games on Roblox. Participants shared their screens while playing games they knew well, narrating their experience in their own words. Each session closed with a co-design exercise asking players to imagine the settings, interactions, and pacing they'd want from a tycoon experience built specifically for them.

My Findings

Players consistently disengaged from games built around mechanical repetition â€” walking between two fixed points, stepping on buttons to advance, waiting for timers to expire. One participant described the difference as loading a song versus playing the piano. What kept players engaged was a sense of authorship: customization they could point to as theirs, problems worth solving, a space that reflected their own decisions. These weren't vague preferences â€” they were specific behavioral patterns with direct design implications that the existing competitive research hadn't surfaced because it wasn't asking the right questions.

How I Delivered My Findings

Every session was open to the studio. Anyone could observe live, add observations to a shared Miro board, or follow along through Slack summaries I posted after each interview. Making the process visible from the start meant findings didn't arrive as a surprise â€” the team was already building intuitions alongside me. Deliverables were tailored by audience: executives received strategic summaries framing the competitive opportunity, design teams received deep dives with behavioral clips and screenshots, and individuals received targeted appendices for their specific product questions. Insights were shared across observation parties, all-hands presentations, standups, and 1:1s throughout the process.

What Was Resolved

The studio's framing shifted from "we need to compete with Roblox" to "Roblox helps us understand our target audience." That reframe changed the nature of the strategic conversation â€” from defensive feature matching to genuine player understanding. The specific findings about authorship, customization, and creative ownership fed directly into feature requirements and sparked design innovations the team hadn't been considering before. EA leadership subsequently adopted this player-focused, decision-driving methodology company-wide, replacing the broad feature audit approach that had been the standard.
