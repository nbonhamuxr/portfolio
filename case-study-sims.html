ELECTRONIC ARTS / MAXIS

How I redefined what research means at EA

A seven-month initiative that moved UXR from a downstream service function to an embedded strategic partner, building the relationships, literacy, and working practices that made research actionable at scale.

My Role
Sr. UX Researcher
Duration
7 months
Reach
Studio-wide: game design, product, and leadership
The Opportunity

Integrating Research Earlier in Product Development

Project Rene was a cross-functional studio of approximately 100 people spanning design, product management, engineering, consumer insights, analytics, and user research. Research was active and valued, but it was most often engaged after teams had already defined the questions they wanted answered.

That structure meant research was primarily supporting decisions rather than shaping how problems were defined, how options were explored, or how direction was formed early in development.

I approached this intentionally. I focused on changing how research fit into the product workflow by working earlier in team conversations, building stronger cross-functional relationships, increasing research literacy, and introducing collaborative practices that brought research into ideation, planning, and decision-making as it happened.

Research Literacy

I started by helping the studio understand what research could do

I co-authored Building For Our Players, a studio-wide series presented alongside the Creative Director, Experience Design Director, and Lead Game PM.

My segment introduced different research methodologies and how to frame research needs around the decisions teams were making, including what they needed to learn and how confident they needed to be to move forward. It emphasized using research earlier in the process to inform and shape direction, not just validate what had been built.

Building Relationships

Changing people's mental model of research one conversation at a time

I intentionally sought out conversations with game designers, producers, PMs, Consumer Insights, and Analytics to understand what they were building, what decisions they were facing, and where uncertainty existed.

During an onsite focus group, I spent time with a game designer after sessions wrapped and walked him through how UX research can support different types of work: evaluative, exploratory, generative, and foundational. We discussed how each approach serves different kinds of product questions depending on where a team is in the development process. That conversation shifted his perspective from seeing research as validation of ideas to something that can help generate and shape them earlier in development. It became a reference point for how I approached similar conversations across the studio.

Making It Collaborative

I shifted research from a service to a shared capability within the studio

As stakeholders began to see how research could be moved earlier in the product process, the next step was inviting them into the research process itself.

I focused on designing research as a shared process from scoping through synthesis, so stakeholders could connect research directly to the decisions they were making and help shape the work as it progressed. A key outcome of this was shifting research from something teams requested into a way of thinking they used when approaching their work.

This took a few forms:

Async Question Scoping

Before a study launched, I opened a shared space where game designers, PMs, and experience designers could contribute their own questions. I introduced a prioritization exercise using a Consequence vs. Desired Confidence framework so the team could align on what mattered most before research started. The research agenda was co-authored by the people who were going to use the findings.

Viewing Parties

I set up live observation sessions where team members could watch participants in real time and add notes to a shared board organized by feature area. Everyone had clear instructions on what to pay attention to, how to take notes, and what to do with what they noticed.

Working Workshops

At an onsite, I replaced findings presentations with working sessions. The team worked through research problems together using dot voting, live synthesis, and collaborative prioritization. They left having made something, not just heard something.

Director's Commentary Debrief

Debriefs were player clips, not slide decks. I played recordings with running commentary covering moments of frustration, delight, and confusion, then had the team synthesize together: what was most surprising, what were the wins, what were the toughest moments. The team built a shared picture.

The Impact

This work changed how the studio identified the game it needed to build

The most visible sign of the shift: a leader who had previously cancelled studies became one of research's most vocal advocates. After watching the whole studio engage with player sessions together during a viewing party, she saw firsthand what research could do when the team was part of it. That moment changed the relationship.

Teams brought UXR in earlier, scoped studies around the questions they actually needed answered, and built planning timelines around what research was learning. Workshop outputs went directly into feature requirement documents. Cross-functional partners showed up in research activities. Findings from UXR, consumer insights, and analytics were regularly combined to inform product direction.

The lasting change was cultural. I built the relationships, literacy, and working practices that made research a strategic function. That gave the organization the infrastructure to make player-centered decisions at scale, and to keep making them long after any individual study was done.

Trust

Research moved from a service request to an ongoing strategic conversation. The team started asking what I needed rather than telling me what to run.

Integration

Workshop outputs were pulled directly into feature requirement documents. Producers and PMs were already part of making sense of what we had learned. They didn't wait for a report.

Partnership

I was invited to review, edit, and co-author feature requirement documents and join product ceremonies as a standing member of the team.

How it unfolded
  • July 2024

    Patient Zero

    An onsite gave me the chance to sit with a game designer at a whiteboard and walk through everything research actually does. We mapped it together: what each method produces, what decisions it can inform, what it cannot answer. One conversation that reframed what was possible.

  • October 2024

    The Onsite

    I ran studio workshops where the team worked on research problems together rather than listening to findings. Dot voting, live synthesis, collaborative prioritization. The team left having made something, not just heard something.

  • November 2024

    Research in the Room

    Producers and PMs started pulling workshop outputs directly into their own feature documents and presentations. Research findings became source material rather than a separate report.

  • December 2024

    The Viewing Party

    Live observation sessions changed the perception of UXR completely. Watching participants in real time, with commentary, landed differently than any readout ever had. Demand for more studies, more involvement, and more of this participatory format came almost immediately.

  • Early 2025

    Invited In

    I was asked to join a product team investigating new gameplay directions as a standing member. I was reviewing and co-authoring feature requirement documents, attending product ceremonies, and proactively proposing research rather than waiting to be asked.
