ELECTRONIC ARTS / MAXIS

How I redefined what research means at EA

A seven-month process of deliberate relationship-building that moved UXR from a service org to an embedded strategic partner — and built the infrastructure to make research actionable at scale.

My Role
Sr. UX Researcher
Duration
7 months
Reach
Studio-wide: game design, product, and leadership
Where Research Lived

UXR had one job: run the playtests

When I joined the game team at Maxis, user research occupied a specific and limited lane. We ran playtests. The team would scope a study request, we'd recruit and moderate, and we'd share what we found. It was a clean service model: they had a question, we had a process.

But that model kept research at the back of the line. Decisions were made in conversations that didn't include us. Findings arrived as presentations — detailed, thorough, and disconnected from the moment when they could have actually changed something. UXR was downstream of everything that mattered.

The Problem

Research was used to evaluate decisions, not inform them

When I joined the team, UXR had a clear but narrow role: run the playtest, share what we found, repeat. Research happened after the important conversations, not during them. Studies were scoped to validate directions that were already set.

I saw an opportunity to change that by building relationships with stakeholders, understanding their pain points, and showing what research could do when it was part of the process from the start.

The Network

I created the conditions for trust before I ever asked for anything

It started with the "Building For Our Players" series — a studio-wide presentation series I co-authored with the Creative Director, Experience Design Director, and Lead Game PM. My segment walked the team through what UXR was learning: not just findings, but how we were learning them and what kinds of questions each method could answer.

Around the same time, I oversaw an onsite focus group and invited game designers and developers to attend as live observers. After one of the sessions ended, I sat down with a game designer who had questions. I listened to what he was working on, where he felt stuck, and what he was uncertain about. I walked him through how UXR could support different types of work — foundational research to understand the space, generative work to explore directions, evaluative work to pressure-test what the team had built. It was a conversation, not a pitch.

That conversation opened the door. I started asking to sit in on product ceremonies — sprint retros, requirement reviews, design crits — not to present, but to listen. To understand what the team was actually deciding so I could proactively surface research that was relevant before anyone had to ask.

The System

I built ways for the team to participate in research, not just receive it

The shift I was trying to make wasn't just about running better studies. It was about changing the relationship between research and the people who needed to act on it. I built structures for co-authoring the research agenda before a study started, tools for observing sessions in real time, and formats for synthesizing findings together rather than receiving a report.

Tool 01

Async Question Scoping

Before a study launched, I opened a shared space where game designers, PMs, and experience designers could add their own questions. A 2x2 framework — Consequence vs. Desired Confidence — helped the whole team prioritize together. By the time research started, the agenda had been co-authored by the people who were going to use the findings.

Tool 02

Viewing Parties

Instead of watching sessions alone and reporting back, I set up structured viewing sessions where team members could observe participants in real time and add notes to a shared board organized by feature area. Non-researchers had clear instructions: what to pay attention to, how to take notes, what to do with what they noticed.

Tool 03

Working Workshops

At an onsite, I replaced findings presentations with working sessions. Rather than presenting to the room, we worked on problems together — dot voting, building on each other's ideas, and turning observations into decisions collaboratively. The team wasn't receiving research. They were doing it.

Tool 04

Director's Commentary Debrief

Debriefs were player clips, not slide decks. I played recordings of participant sessions with running commentary — real moments of frustration, delight, and confusion — and had the team synthesize together: what was most surprising, what were the wins, what were the toughest moments. The team built a shared picture rather than inheriting mine.

The Turn

Seven months from a whiteboard conversation to a standing seat at the table

The shift didn't happen all at once. It built through a series of specific moments that each moved the relationship forward.

  • July 2024

    Patient Zero

    An onsite gave me the chance to sit with a game designer at a whiteboard and walk through everything research actually does. We mapped it together: what each method produces, what decisions it can inform, what it can't answer. One conversation that reframed what was possible.

  • October 2024

    The Onsite

    I ran studio workshops where the team worked on research problems together rather than listening to findings. Dot voting, live synthesis, collaborative prioritization. The team left having made something, not just heard something.

  • November 2024

    Research in the Room

    Producers and PMs started pulling workshop outputs directly into their own feature documents and presentations. Research findings became source material — not a separate report sitting in a Slack channel.

  • December 2024

    The Viewing Party

    Live observation sessions changed the game manager's perception of UXR completely. Watching participants in real time, with commentary, landed differently than any readout ever had. Demand for more studies, more involvement, and more of this participatory format came almost immediately.

  • Early 2025

    Invited In

    I was asked to join a product team investigating new gameplay directions as a standing member — not a vendor brought in when needed. I was reviewing and co-authoring feature requirement documents, attending product ceremonies, and proactively proposing research rather than waiting to be asked.

The Enduring Impact

Research moved from "they run our playtests" to "what do you need from us"

By early 2025, the relationship had changed fundamentally. I wasn't being asked to run studies on request — I was being asked what I needed to run them, so the team could build their sprints around it. I was reviewing feature requirement documents. I was in product ceremonies. I was co-authoring the documentation that shaped what the team built next.

Trust

Research moved from a service request to an ongoing strategic conversation. The team started asking what I needed rather than telling me what to run.

Integration

Workshop outputs were pulled directly into feature requirement documents. Producers and PMs were already part of making sense of what we'd learned — they didn't wait for a report.

Partnership

I was invited to review, edit, and co-author feature requirement documents and join product ceremonies as a standing member of the team — not a guest.
