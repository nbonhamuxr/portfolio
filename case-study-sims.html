ELECTRONIC ARTS / MAXIS — PROJECT RENE

Aligning a Studio Around Who a Mobile-First Sims Player Actually Is

Controls Ease-of-Use
+55% score improvement
Studio Impact
Studio-wide shift toward mobile-first players
Research Model
Cross-team workshops and playtest viewings
Background

01

Project Rene is a mobile-first, multiplayer concept within The Sims franchise — a significant departure from the franchise's PC-native roots. For Maxis, this meant designing for two fundamentally different audiences at once: longtime Sims fans with deeply ingrained expectations about how the game looks, feels, and works, and an entirely new population of mobile-first players who had never touched The Sims before. The franchise had decades of institutional knowledge built around the first group. Almost none of it applied to the second.

The Problem

02

Without a clear, shared picture of who the mobile-first, new-to-franchise player actually was, development decisions risked defaulting to what the studio already knew. UI patterns designed for mouse-and-keyboard. Pacing assumptions shaped by players who had played every Sims title since the early 2000s. The business risk wasn't just a bad product for a new audience — it was a missed opportunity to grow a franchise into a new generation of players who were already playing on the platforms The Sims needed to reach.

My Approach

03

I combined end-to-end quantitative and qualitative research — covering UI, gameplay, controls, visual style, fashion systems, and multiplayer frameworks — to build a research-driven narrative about who this new player actually was: their mental models, their expectations coming in from mobile gaming, and what they valued differently from a longtime fan. The harder part was making that narrative stick across a full studio, not just one team. I worked inside the existing agile rhythm — sprint planning, retrospectives, requirement sessions — to keep research integrated with development as the work evolved, rather than arriving as a periodic report from the outside.

My Findings

04

Mobile-first players approached the game with fundamentally different mental models. They expected controls to feel intuitive without a learning curve, because mobile games they already loved had taught them to expect that. They engaged with fashion and customization differently — as expression, not as a completionist system to unlock. And they experienced multiplayer not as a feature bolted onto a single-player game, but as the reason to play. These weren't small differences in preference. They were differences in how the game needed to be designed from the ground up to feel right to someone encountering the franchise for the first time.

How I Delivered My Findings

05

I built a participatory research culture around the work so insight didn't depend on me being in the room. Cross-team scoping workshops brought research and design together before studies launched. Studio-wide playtest viewing sessions let anyone observe research in progress and form their own reactions to what they saw. I shared insights across multiple channels — 1:1s, Slack, show-and-tells, and studio-wide all-hands — so different audiences could engage at the depth that was useful to them. I also coached teams directly on reading their own behavioral data in BigQuery and Looker, so the teams could track the questions research raised without waiting for me to run another study.

What Was Resolved

06

The studio's development culture shifted to genuinely account for mobile-first, new-to-franchise players — not as an afterthought layered onto existing design, but as a population shaping decisions from the start. The controls work alone raised the ease-of-use score by 55%, driven directly by addressing the usability gaps and expectation mismatches specific to mobile-first players. More broadly, the research established a shared language across the studio for talking about two different audiences — and gave teams a way to make that distinction in real decisions, not just in principle.
