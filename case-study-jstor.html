01

BACKGROUND

JSTOR and Artstor were being merged into a single unified platform under ITHAKA, serving over 150 million visitors a year. JSTOR was built for text-based scholarly research. Artstor was built for visual art discovery — used primarily by art history faculty and students who needed to find, organize, and teach with images. Combining them wasn't just a technical challenge. It required understanding how two very different kinds of scholars worked and what they needed from an experience that had never existed before.

02

THE PROBLEM

After more than a year, the integration had stalled. The two product teams each had their own roadmap, their own definition of success, and no shared vocabulary for what "done" meant. Decisions went in circles. Meetings ended with more disagreement than they started with. For ITHAKA, this wasn't just an internal friction problem — it was a real business risk. A platform serving millions of academics couldn't afford to stay incomplete indefinitely, and the longer the two teams stayed misaligned, the more expensive every decision became.

03

MY APPROACH

Rather than running another round of research aimed at proving one team's roadmap right, I stepped back and asked a more fundamental question: what outcomes did users actually need from this merged platform, independent of either team's existing plans? I combined usability testing, surveys, Google Analytics, and A/B testing to build a User Outcomes Framework — research-informed statements that expressed user goals in terms of their lives and careers, not just actions within the product. Alongside it, I created The Story of Art History: a narrative presentation that oriented JSTOR staff — most of whom had limited familiarity with Artstor's users — to how art history scholars actually work and why it matters to them.

04

MY FINDINGS

Users across both platforms shared underlying needs that crossed the existing organizational divide. One example: when studying how users "look for inspiration," A/B testing revealed that a "Load more" button significantly outperformed both pagination and infinite scroll. Not because of a preference — because of a behavior. Users doing exploratory search needed to control the pace of discovery without losing their place. That kind of finding wasn't about features; it was about how scholars think and move through information. Translating those behaviors into user outcomes gave both teams a shared language they could actually use to evaluate competing priorities.

05

HOW I DELIVERED MY FINDINGS

I brought the framework into the room with both teams together — not as a finished artifact to approve, but as a working draft to pressure-test. A collaborative Google Sheets scorecard tracked outcomes and let both teams see how their work stacked up against the same yardstick in real time. I designed the conversation as carefully as I designed the research: the goal was for both teams to leave the room believing the framework was theirs, not mine. That shift in ownership mattered more than any individual finding in it.

06

WHAT WAS RESOLVED

The framework gave the integration a shared finish line for the first time. It directly shaped a major redesign of JSTOR's content viewer, which raised platform ease-of-use by 16%. More durably, the framework was adopted org-wide as the standard way ITHAKA's product teams ground decisions in real user needs — well beyond the original integration project that prompted it.

"What began as a research artifact became a unifying framework used across product teams to anchor decisions in real user needs." — Former manager, Director of User Insights, ITHAKA
