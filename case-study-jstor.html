The situation

JSTOR and Artstor were merging into a single platform serving over 150 million visitors a year, but the two product teams had spent more than a year unable to agree on what the merged experience should actually prioritize. Each team had its own roadmap, its own definition of success, and no shared way to talk about what "done" meant. Decisions stalled in meetings that ended with more disagreement than they started with.

What I did

Rather than running another round of research aimed at proving one team's roadmap right, I stepped back and asked a different question: what outcomes did users actually need from this merged platform, independent of either team's existing plan? I combined usability testing, surveys, Google Analytics, and A/B testing to build a user-outcomes framework — a shared, evidence-based scorecard that named the outcomes that mattered and let both teams measure their work against the same yardstick.

I brought that framework into the room with both teams together, not as a finished deliverable to approve, but as a working draft to pressure-test. That mattered as much as the framework itself — the goal was for both teams to leave believing it was theirs, not mine.

The outcome

The framework gave the integration a shared finish line for the first time. It directly shaped a major redesign of JSTOR's content viewer, which raised platform ease-of-use by 16%. More lasting than that one redesign, the framework itself was adopted org-wide as the standard way ITHAKA's product teams ground decisions in real user needs, well beyond the original integration project.

"What began as a research artifact became a unifying framework used across product teams to anchor decisions in real user needs." Former manager — Director of User Insights, ITHAKA
